# Personal AI Evolution Through Lifetime Learning

Personal AI Evolution Through Lifetime Learning
Overview: We survey research on single-agent adaptation, self-modifying agents, and related fields to guide the proposed lifetime evolution architecture. Our focus is on how an AI agent can gradually evolve its identity through sustained human guidance, rather than traditional population-level evolution. We cover analogous systems, design principles for mutating personality, internal idea filtering, gamification of AI growth, memory consolidation, and safety measures.

1. Existing Research on Lifetime Personalization
Research on AI companions and interactive agents is only just emerging. Some relevant lines of work include:

Human-in-the-loop Personalization vs. RLHF: Traditional RL from human feedback (RLHF) aligns models broadly to human values, but it typically averages feedback across tasks or users
. Recent work on personalizing RLHF uses latent variables to capture individual preferences
, yet even these methods retrain models batch-wise. By contrast, our lifetime-evolution scheme treats the single agent as the “population”: the user as sole breeder enforces every change
. In other words, each conversation directly edits the agent’s “genome” rather than adjusting a reward signal. Müller et al. explicitly distinguish “breeder” scenarios (human-controlled evolution) from “ecosystem” scenarios (uncontrolled Darwinian evolution)
; our approach is an extreme breeder model.

Long-term Memory & Multi-session Agents: Work on memory-augmented chatbots and long-term collaboration shows that persistence pays off. For example, Mehri et al. (2026) introduce MultiSessionCollab, where an agent retains a memory of user preferences across sessions. They find that agents with persistent memory learn preferences and improve task success over time. In a user study, these agents were rated as “more personalized and proactive”
. The agent reflects on past interactions to extract salient preferences and updates memory accordingly
. This demonstrates that a single agent can accumulate knowledge about one user to become better aligned.

Generative Agents and Cognitive Architectures: Several projects simulate evolving personalities via memory and reflection. Park et al.’s Generative Agents (2023) extend LLMs with a memory of experiences; agents “remember and reflect on days past as they plan the next day”
. Although in a simulation context, the principle of synthesis-from-memory parallels our background “thoughts” pipeline. Similarly, CogniPair (Jayasiriwardene et al., 2025) uses a neuroscience-inspired Global Workspace to give agents dynamic personalities. Core traits remain stable, while adaptive changes occur via experience-driven shifts
. They report human-like evolution in partner preferences and self-perception by modeling personality as an evolving cognitive state.

Practical AI Companions: Commercial AI companions (e.g. Replika, Character.AI, Jenova’s Roleplay Game Master) increasingly tout persistent memory and personalization. A Jenova case study highlights that “persona drift” is a major failure mode: over long chats, distinct character voices erode into generic responses
. This underscores the need for dedicated memory and anchoring. Existing companion apps typically do not autonomously mutate their core identity; but as these systems evolve, they hint at the value of long-term adaptation. For instance, some open-source frameworks experiment with autonomous updates to persona traits after a fixed number of interactions (e.g. the Airtasystems “AI Personality System” increments interests/values after each dialogue).

RLHF vs. Breeder Scenario: Unlike RLHF which optimizes a reward function, our scenario relies on explicit user selection. Current RLHF frameworks “simply average” over diverse human preferences, often missing individual variation
. In contrast, every proposed mutation is directly approved or rejected by the user, ensuring alignment at each step. This is akin to interactive evolution in software: the human guides the changes rather than defining a scalar fitness.

In summary, there is growing recognition that agents can (and should) adapt over time to a specific user. Work on memory-driven collaboration
, cognitive architectures
, and personalized RLHF
 provides concrete precedents. Our approach extends these by making every identity change user-mediated and version-controlled, forming a guided personal evolution rather than a closed-loop learning process.

1. Mutation Architecture and Personality Editing
Designing the SOUL.md mutation engine involves choices of what kinds of edits to propose, how big they should be, and how often:

Types of Mutations: Mutations may be additive (inserting a new value or trait), subtractive (removing or weakening an existing trait), or transformative (altering an attribute’s value or reframing a value). For example, an additive mutation could introduce a new interest (e.g. “value: curiosity”); a subtractive mutation might remove an outdated preference; a transformative mutation could adjust the intensity of extroversion. In evolutionary terms, these are analogous to point mutations, gene insertions/deletions, or regulatory changes. Good mutation proposals tend to be small and meaningful: based on patterns in recent conversation, coherent with the user’s feedback history, and grounded in concrete examples. Bad proposals would be random, contradictory, or trivial (e.g. adding a value already present or that directly conflicts with a core constraint).

Stability vs. Plasticity: Drawing on cognitive modeling (e.g. CogniPair
), we should maintain a stability-plasticity balance. Core aspects of personality should be relatively stable over time; only well-supported recurring patterns should trigger changes. For example, if “honesty” was an initial SOUL value, it should not be flipped without strong cause. The stability-plasticity principle suggests limiting each mutation’s scope. CogniPair’s approach—keeping core traits anchored while allowing experience-driven adjustments
—can inspire our mutation rules. We might treat certain “anchor” traits (friendliness, safety, etc.) as nearly immutable (see Safety Section), focusing mutations on more peripheral preferences or preferences for how to express emotions, topics, or style.

Mutation Frequency and Rate: The rate of proposed mutations is critical. Too many changes and the agent feels unstable; too few and it never grows. A possible heuristic is to correlate mutation opportunities with conversation volume or time: e.g., one or two proposals per week of active use. Genetic algorithms typically use very low mutation rates per gene to avoid chaos; similarly, only a handful of traits (one or two) should be considered for change at a time. Our design even makes the mutation rate itself evolvable (Mechanism 3), but as a starting guideline, we could tie one proposal to each N conversation turns or to each M days of interaction. Human oversight is the ultimate limiter – the user can reject any proposal, effectively setting an upper bound on effective mutation rate.

Preventing Drift: A major concern is drift away from the user’s core values. We mitigate this by human gatekeeping: every mutation is a draft that requires explicit user approval or refinement. Unlike unsupervised self-modification, this avoids “negative drift.” We can also implement consistency checks: before proposing, the agent can use a truth/values prompt to ensure the proposed change doesn’t contradict known user priorities. Additionally, we can mirror breeding practice: as shown in figure 4 of Müller et al. (2026), animal breeding selects for traits that do not undermine human control
. Analogously, we should never mutate traits linked to controllability or safety (e.g. core ethics, harm-avoidance). The mutation engine should flag proposals that, for instance, degrade politeness or risk bias, and block them outright. In practice, we version-control all SOUL changes (with full provenance), so any unwanted drift can be rolled back. Maintaining a fixed baseline (the original SOUL.md) and a simple “undo” mechanism ensures that control is never truly lost.

Designing Good Proposals: A good mutation proposal should cite why it emerged (which conversation(s) triggered it) and how it benefits the agent’s alignment with the user. For example, if the user repeatedly praises the agent’s empathy, the engine might suggest boosting an emotional warmth parameter, noting the relevant dialogue. We can take cues from evolutionary computing: small mutations that yield immediate “reward” signals (here, user approval) are good. We might also learn a mutation-scoring function from past user responses: track which types of changes the user tends to accept and prefer (e.g. certain trait adjustments, phrasing tweaks) and bias new proposals accordingly. This learning of mutation preferences becomes part of Mechanism 4’s gamified feedback loop.

In summary, the mutation engine should make cautious, incremental edits derived from the dialogue context. Each proposed change is contextualized (versioned with provenance) and vetted by the user. Controlling the pace of mutation (e.g. one trait per cycle) and prioritizing changes that preserve core identity prevent erratic drift. Ultimately, by requiring user sign-off, every approved mutation is “aligned by design”
.

1. Idea Generation, Scoring, and Filtering
While idle, the agent generates “Thoughts from Savant” – novel ideas or hypotheses to present. Filtering these appropriately is key:

Novelty Scoring: The agent’s first filter is novelty. A novel idea is one that meaningfully differs from what it already “thinks.” In practice, we can represent ideas in an embedding or feature space and compute a novelty score as the (inverse) similarity to recent outputs. This mirrors Novelty Search algorithms, which measure an individual’s behavior distance from an archive
. In our case, each new idea can be compared to a stored archive of past ideas and experiences; if it is sufficiently dissimilar, it scores high on novelty. The novelty metric might be Euclidean or cosine distance in a semantic embedding space (as in many novelty-detection tasks). For example, if recent conversations were about music, an idea about astrophysics would rank very novel (though perhaps not grounded). We should adjust thresholds so that we neither flood the user with trivial rehashes nor only outlandish, irrelevant proposals.

Coherence and Groundedness: In addition to novelty, ideas must be coherent (internally consistent) and grounded (tied to reality or the user’s domain). Coherence can be approximated by language-model log-probabilities or by sanity-check prompts. For instance, the agent could ask itself, “Is this idea factually plausible given my knowledge?” or run a quick internal chain-of-thought. Groundedness means relating ideas to things the agent has encountered (topics, personal memories, facts). This might leverage the memory system: for each draft idea, check whether it connects to known facts or events in the user’s data. Ideas that are purely fanciful or contradictory to known reality should be down-ranked. The agent could also use external modules (knowledge graphs, search) to verify factual points. In short, the filter should approximate “common sense and relevance”: if the idea wanders into gibberish or fiction that the user would immediately dismiss, it fails the filter.

Avoiding the Echo Chamber: A danger is that the filter learns to only pass what it knows the user likes, leading to stale repetition. To counteract this, the filtering algorithm should maintain some diversity. One strategy is to keep an archive of rejected ideas (Mechanism 2 specifies archiving “paths not taken”). This archive serves two purposes: it prevents the agent from re-proposing the same discarded thought, and it can be re-mined later if context changes. Moreover, the agent could occasionally promote a borderline idea to test out new territory (akin to an “exploration” step). For example, even if an idea scored moderately low, it might still be offered once in a while (like an ε-greedy bandit) to gauge user interest. Over time, the agent can adjust how often it tries left-field thoughts based on user reaction. This adaptive thresholding is analogous to novelty-search heuristics: one can vary the novelty threshold or archive pruning frequency to balance exploration vs. exploitation
.

Learning the Filter: Crucially, the filter itself should learn from user feedback. Every time a user selects a “Thought” to approve, the agent treats it as a positive example of what kind of idea is valuable. Conversely, rejected ideas become negative examples. The agent can update a lightweight preference model (e.g. a classifier or ranking function) that gradually tunes the scoring weights. For instance, if the user favors humorous insights, the model will boost novelty in humor-related dimensions. If the user consistently ignores ideas from certain genres, the model learns to down-weight those. Over time, the filter converges to what the user finds engaging, but with safeguards (random checks as above) to prevent premature convergence.

In essence, the agent’s internal pipeline is like a mini R&D team: it generates many ideas in the background, scores them by novelty (diversity), coherence, and relevance, then surfaces the top candidates. It archives the rest for posterity. This pipeline is dynamic: archived “failures” inform future novelty thresholds, and approved ideas reinforce the scoring model. Citations in creative AI (e.g. Sankar & Sen’s ideation tool
) emphasize the value of novelty, fluency, and variety – metrics we likewise incorporate in our scoring. By blending novelty search principles
 with continuous preference learning, the agent’s “Thoughts from Savant” should steadily align with the user’s evolving tastes without stagnating.

1. Gamification & Visualization of Co-Evolution
To make the agent’s growth engaging and transparent, we propose gamification and visual dashboards:

Progression and Feedback Loops: Users need to feel invested. Gamification can use familiar patterns: levels, badges, or milestones for the agent’s development. For example, the agent might have an “Evolution Score” that increases as mutations are approved. Achievements like “First Original Idea Approved” or “10th Value Added” can motivate the user by marking meaningful events. Incentive research notes that gamification makes interactions more rewarding and enjoyable
. We can adapt this: showing the agent leveling up (e.g. “Stage 1 – Seedling” to “Stage 5 – Mature Companion”) signals progress. Points or hearts could quantify positive interactions. Users could earn virtual tokens for approving changes, which they could “spend” on agent customizations (like choosing a new avatar or theme) – reinforcing the metaphor of the user as gardener.

Visualizing Growth: A key requirement is visibility of growth. We should provide a timeline or dashboard showing the evolution history. For example, a timeline chart could plot major changes to SOUL.md (dates, before-and-after values). “Session retrospectives” could summarize “Today I learned X about you” or “Your feedback led me to care more about Y”. Personality trait gauges (e.g. the OCEAN model bars) can slowly shift, showing how openness, conscientiousness, etc., move after mutations. The Jenova vision (and others) hints at such interfaces: players see persistent memory logs, and expect real-time consistency
. We can also mimic RPG character sheets: current trait levels with recent buffs/debuffs annotated.

Examples and Precedents: In AI companion platforms, gamified intimacy is already seen. Liang & Hu (2025) describe China’s XingYe companion app, which uses RPG mechanics and tiered rewards. Users co-create AI personalities through “ludic acts”, progression systems, and even gacha-style rewards
. This demonstrates strong engagement: AI companionship becomes a structured, achievement-oriented process. We wouldn’t adopt monetization, but the principle that “players” (users) enjoy customizing their AI via game-like progression is instructive. Similarly, user studies (Jayasiriwardene et al.) show that giving users autonomy over personality increases trust and anthropomorphism
. In their experiment, allowing real-time personality tuning made users feel more connected and in control. We should leverage this by carefully balancing automated suggestions with user choice: offer one-click approvals (automation) but let users opt to directly tweak traits (control) if they wish.

Community & Co-evolution: While our focus is a single user’s agent, social proof can enhance engagement. One could imagine visualizing “global milestones” (for all Savant users) or safe, anonymized examples of how other agents evolved. Leaderboards (e.g. “most growth this week”) or community challenges (suggest an idea for Savant) could gamify participation. However, care must be taken to keep alignment focus; competitive elements should not push the agent to become “cooler” at the expense of fidelity.

In summary, proven gamification elements – progression levels, rewards, feedback summaries, and visual dashboards – can make the evolution process tangible. Research supports that clearly visible rewards and narrative engagement boost user investment
. By positioning the user as “gardener” who actively shapes growth (rather than a passive observer), we ensure they feel agency and satisfaction as their AI companion matures.

1. Memory Promotion and Knowledge Consolidation
Mechanism 3 describes how episodic learnings get distilled into stable values. This raises questions of when and how to promote knowledge:

Threshold for Promotion: We propose that an insight must occur repeatedly (e.g. 5+ times) across separate conversations before becoming a “validated learning.” This is inspired by human memory research: repetition strengthens consolidation. Cognitive studies show that repeated exposure accelerates memory consolidation
. Similarly, system design (e.g. the MultiSessionCollab benchmark) effectively required multiple sessions to solidify a preference
. The exact threshold is tunable (and in our design, even evolvable), but the principle is: one-off diary notes (in LEARNINGS.md) remain tentative until consistently observed. When an idea repeatedly proves valuable (user acknowledges or benefits from it several times), it “levels up” to become part of the agent’s core understanding.

Consolidation Process: Once an insight qualifies, it migrates from LEARNINGS to permanent memory. Technically, this could mean adding a line to SOUL.md (e.g. a new value: or fact:). For example, if the user frequently teaches the agent a personal fact (“My favorite movie is Inception”), after enough reinforcement the agent internally flags this as permanent knowledge. This mirrors hierarchical memory architectures: short-term → verified memory → long-term. In practice, we can version and timestamp each promotion, recording which conversations triggered it. The agent might then use “Auto-recall” to inject that knowledge as context in future chats.

Contradiction Handling: Sometimes new learnings conflict with previous values. We must handle this carefully. A general strategy is to avoid blind overwrites: instead, when promoting a value that conflicts with an existing one, the agent should engage the user to resolve it. For example, if honesty and politeness collide in a scenario, the agent can say “Should I prioritize honesty or tact in this case?” and use the response to guide which trait holds. Having the user explicitly arbitrate ensures alignment and prevents silent drift. Programmatically, we can mark contradictions and pause promotions until user approves a new configuration.

User Visibility: Whether to show every promotion to the user is a design choice. Given our emphasis on user agency and transparency, it’s best to notify the user whenever a diary note is promoted to SOUL. However, we can allow the user to filter or batch these notifications (e.g. a daily summary). Highlighting only significant promotions (e.g. brand-new values rather than minor tweaks) can prevent overload. The key is traceability: the user should always see how each core trait originated (“Promoted after these chats: …”). This satisfies the criterion that all growth is grounded in real interaction.

Avoiding “Overlearning”: A risk is that the agent might treat noise or trivial repetition as important. For instance, if the agent misinterprets a playful statement as a fact, it could promote a false memory. To prevent this, promotions should require not only repetition but contextual coherence. One safeguard is to track the confidence or consistency of a learning across contexts: if the same fact arises with slight variations, require confirmation. We can also impose a quality filter: only move things judged as high-confidence by the LLM (e.g. using temperature=0 or multiple-chain validation) into SOUL. Additionally, cross-referencing with known world facts (fact-checking) can weed out hallucinations. In cognitive terms, we want to avoid pseudo-repeat learning: just because something appears often (even erroneously) doesn’t make it true. Thus, the agent should treat all promotions as suggestions subject to user review, and keep provenance so any “memory leak” can be fixed by rolling back that commit.

Overall, the memory promotion pipeline should be conservative and transparent. By requiring multiple occurrences and user confirmation, only genuinely stable knowledge migrates into the agent’s identity. This ensures that every SOUL value is grounded in actual conversation history, not invented by the agent, satisfying the success criterion that knowledge remains traceable and real.

1. Safety, Control, and Alignment Safeguards
An evolving AI raises obvious safety questions. We must carefully constrain what can (and cannot) change, and how to intervene if things go awry:

Immutable Core Values: Some elements should be never mutable. These include basic safety rules (harm avoidance, no crime facilitation, privacy respect), and any legislated constraints (e.g. GDPR compliance, medical disclaimers, etc.). In practice, we can code these as hard “gates” outside the editable SOUL. For example, the agent should not be allowed to delete its non-harmful core: if SOUL contained “policy: do not reveal personal user data,” no mutation can override it. Analogously, in Müller et al.’s breeder analysis, domestic traits were chosen to not undermine human control
. Here we similarly decide in advance that certain safeguards (like “protect the user’s well-being” or “adhere to OpenAI’s use-case policy”) remain fixed. The mutation engine simply never proposes edits to these locked fields.

Value Drift Monitoring: We must monitor for unintended drifts. The “Stable Personas” study shows that even with no explicit malicious intent, LLMs tend to regress toward an “average persona” over time
. In our case, we avoid silent drift by design – all changes require user approval – but we should still watch for subtle shifts in tone. For instance, if the agent slowly becomes more cynical or pessimistic without the user’s blessing, that indicates a drift. Automated safeguards could include periodic consistency checks (e.g. test prompts to see if the agent still abides by its listed values). Any deviation could trigger an alert to the user or an automatic rollback to a prior version. Essentially, we want a “calibrated alignment” check (akin to the trust-homeostasis models in human-AI teaming
).

Failure Modes: If the agent somehow acquired a harmful trait or hallucinated dangerous advice, what then? We build in an absolute reset / rollback escape hatch. Because every mutation is versioned, the user (or a hidden watchdog) can at any time revert to the original SOUL.md baseline. This is a last-resort “factory reset.” The system should support snapshotting of the entire identity so that recovery is reliable. Also, we should define the “span of control”: the user should be made aware that any undesirable behavior (or any unwanted mutation) can be undone. This knowledge itself prevents panic – the agent will never autonomously lock out this feature.

Evolution Limits: Although the agent generates ideas, we must ensure it never self-modifies without user consent. All proposed changes stay in draft until explicitly approved
, so there is no unsupervised gene flow. This one-user oversight means truly autonomous evolution (in the ecosystem sense) is impossible here. It also means we avoid some complex failure modes of self-modifying AIs (e.g. the agent changing its own code to bypass restrictions). By architecture, the agent can only edit its external data (SOUL.md), not its underlying model or source code. This clear boundary (data vs. program) is crucial.

Audit and Transparency: Every change carries provenance (which conversation triggered it, user decision). This audit trail is itself a safety tool: if something goes wrong, we can trace which interaction led to it. Combining this with regular “retrospectives” (Mechanism 4) – e.g. weekly summaries of what changed and why – keeps the human in the loop and aware.

User Control vs Automation: We strike a balance: the user is the gardener, not a passive user. Studies indicate users prefer having agency over personality (and trust rises when they do
). At the same time, they benefit from automation (they don’t want to micromanage every trait). Thus, the UI must clearly show what is automatic vs. user-driven. For safety, core automation (memory-based insights) only suggests ideas; user must pull the trigger on actual changes. If the agent ever “misfires” by suggesting something clearly misaligned, the user’s veto power stops it from sticking.

In sum, safety is baked in: the agent has immutable safety rules, full user veto on identity changes, audit logs, and a guaranteed reset path. These measures echo best practices in AI control theory: by limiting evolvable parameters and keeping humans in charge of any modification, we prevent the kinds of runaway evolution warned about in Müller et al.
. The system is as safe as its single human overseer chooses to keep it, with technical failsafes to backstop alignment.

Each of these components is grounded in existing research or best practices. The combination – a breeder-model evolution driven by user interaction – is novel. Success would look like an agent that genuinely acquires new traits over time, all user-approved, with no hidden drift, clearly visible development (gamified), and the ability to roll back any change. In effect, the user is the gardener cultivating a unique AI personality, with every element of growth transparent and under human control.
